CYBERCRIME AWARENESS

What is Cybercrime?

Cybercrime is any illegal activity that involves a computer, network, or the internet— either as a tool to commit the crime or as the target of the crime.
Cybercrime refers to criminal activities that involve the use of computers, networks, or the internet. It can include activities such as hacking, identity theft, online fraud, and the distribution of malicious software.

Types of Cybercrime

There are several types of cybercrime, including:

  • Financial Crimes
  • Privacy & Personal Data Crimes
  • Harassment & Emotional Crimes
  • Device & System Crimes
  • Identity & Impersonation Crimes

Financial Crimes

  1. UPI / Banking Fraud

    2. UPI / Banking Fraud is a type of cybercrime in which criminals use deception, fake messages, fraudulent calls, phishing links, fake UPI requests, or stolen banking information to illegally access a person's bank account or transfer their money without permission.
    • The criminal creates urgency, fear, or confusion so the victim acts without checking.
    • The victim is pushed to reveal an OTP, UPI PIN, card information, or to approve a payment request.
    • Some scams begin with a fake customer-care number, refund offer, delivery problem, KYC warning, or account-blocking message.
    • A legitimate bank or UPI service does not need your UPI PIN or OTP to send money to you.

    Warning signs:

    • "Act within 10 minutes or your account will be blocked."
    • Requests to install remote-access/screen-sharing applications.
    • A request to enter a UPI PIN while supposedly receiving money.
    • Payment requests from unknown UPI IDs or QR codes.

    Prevention:

    • Never share your UPI PIN, OTP, or card information with anyone.
    • Always verify the source of any message or call before taking action.
    • Be cautious of urgent requests for money or personal information.
    • Use strong, unique passwords for your banking and UPI accounts.

    What to do if you are a victim:

    • Contact your bank/payment provider immediately and request that the transaction/account be secured.
    • Save transaction IDs, screenshots, phone numbers, messages, and other evidence.
    • Report the incident through the appropriate cybercrime/bank reporting channel as soon as possible.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. OTP Fraud

      2. -Fraudster calls pretending to be bank employee

      -Asks for OTP saying "to verify your account"

      Example: "Your SBI account will be blocked, share OTP to continue"

    2. QR Code Scam

      3. -Sends fake QR code saying "scan to receive money"

      -But scanning sends money instead of receiving

      Example: OLX seller sends QR — victim scans and loses ₹10,000

    3. Fake UPI Handle

      4. -Creates handle like sbi.care@upi to look official

      -Sends fake payment request

      Example: Fake BHIM/GPay support asks to verify by sending ₹1

    4. Screen Sharing Fraud

      5. -Asks to install AnyDesk or TeamViewer for "help"

      -Watches your screen and steals banking details

      Example: Fake Amazon refund agent accesses your phone remotely

  2. Online Fraud / Scams

    3. Online Fraud / Scams illegal or deceptive activities carried out through the internet, websites, social media, emails, messaging apps, or online platforms to trick people into giving away their money, personal information, passwords, OTPs, or account details.

    How the scam usually works:

    • The fraudster creates an attractive opportunity such as a job, prize, discount, relationship, or easy-income scheme.
    • Trust is built first; the victim is then asked for money, identity documents, login information, or access to a device.
    • Small initial payments may be used to make the scheme appear genuine before a larger payment is demanded.

    Warning signs:

    • Guaranteed income or unusually high returns with little/no effort.
    • Upfront "registration", "processing", "security", or "GST" fees.

    Additional Warning Signs:

    • Pressure to communicate only through Telegram/WhatsApp.
    • Refusal to provide verifiable company information or a physical address.
    • Fake reviews, copied logos, or websites with suspicious domains.

    Prevention:

    • Independently verify the company, offer, website, and contact details.
    • Never pay money simply to receive a job, prize, refund, or guaranteed earning opportunity.

    Additional Prevention Tips:

    • Avoid sharing Aadhaar/PAN, bank details, or selfies until the organization is verified.
    • Do not trust screenshots of payments or profits as proof of legitimacy.

    Impact:

    Financial loss, identity theft, exposure of personal information, and emotional stress can occur together.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Job Scam

      2. -Fake job offer with high salary, asks registration fee

      Example: "Work from home, earn ₹50,000/month - pay ₹500 to register"

    2. Lottery / Prize Scam

      3. -Message says you won iPhone or cash prize

      -Asks for processing fee to claim

      Example: "You won KBC lucky draw - pay ₹2,000 GST to claim ₹10 lakh"

    3. Shopping Scam

      4. -Fake e-commerce website sells products but never delivers

      Example: Fake Nike website at 80% discount - payment done, no delivery

    4. Part-time Job Scam

      5. -"Like YouTube videos and earn ₹500 per task"

      -Works initially, then asks for investment - vanishes

      Example: Telegram group promises ₹2,000/day for simple tasks

    5. Matrimonial Scam

      6. -Fake profile on shaadi.com, builds trust then asks money

      Example: "I'm stuck abroad, send ₹20,000 for flight ticket"

  3. Fake Loan Apps

    4. Fake Loan Apps are fraudulent mobile applications that pretend to offer loans or financial services but are designed to steal personal information, money, or both from unsuspecting users.

    How the fraud usually works:

    • A fake or predatory loan application advertises instant loans with minimal documentation.
    • During installation or registration, it may request excessive permissions such as contacts, SMS, microphone, storage, or photos.
    • After disbursing a small amount, the operator may impose hidden charges, extremely aggressive repayment demands, or unauthorized deductions.
    • Stolen contacts and photographs can then be used for intimidation and blackmail.

    Warning signs:

    • Loan approval without normal verification.
    • Excessive permissions unrelated to lending.
    • Pressure to pay immediately to avoid contacting friends/family.
    • Threatening messages from unknown numbers.
    • App links received through unsolicited SMS, WhatsApp, Telegram, or social media.

    Prevention:

    • Install financial apps only from trusted sources and verify the lender's identity and regulatory status where applicable.
    • Review permissions before installation.
    • Do not provide contacts/gallery access merely because an app demands it.
    • Read the complete loan terms, interest, fees, repayment schedule, and lender details before accepting a loan.

    If harassed:

    • Do not engage in prolonged arguments with threatening callers.
    • Preserve screenshots, call logs, payment records, app details, and messages.
    • Remove suspicious applications only after preserving necessary evidence and securing important accounts.
    • Inform trusted family members so they are not misled by the fraudster.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Data Theft at Install

      2. -App asks permission to access contacts, gallery, SMS

      Example: App named "QuickRupee" accesses all your photos at install

    2. Harassment After Loan

      3. -Charges hidden interest, then calls/messages abusively

      Example: Borrowed ₹5,000 - asked to repay ₹15,000 in 7 days

    3. Morphed Image Blackmail

      4. -Uses your photo from gallery, morphs it and sends to contacts

      Example: Fake obscene photo sent to your family members via WhatsApp

    4. Recovery Agent Threats

      5. -Fake agents call with abusive language and death threats

      Example: "We will come to your house if you don't pay in 2 hours"

  4. Investment Scam

    5. Investment Scam is a type of fraud where scammers trick individuals into investing money in fake or misleading investment opportunities, promising high returns with little or no risk.

    How the scam usually works:

    • Fraudsters create a professional-looking investment app, website, social-media page, or chat group.
    • Fake dashboards may display rapidly increasing profits even though no real investment exists.
    • Victims are encouraged to deposit progressively larger amounts.
    • When the victim attempts withdrawal, the scammer may demand a "tax", "unlock fee", "verification fee", or additional deposit.

    Warning signs:

    • Guaranteed or unusually high returns.
    • Claims of secret insider information or risk-free trading.
    • Pressure to invest immediately.
    • Requests to transfer money to personal accounts or unrelated wallets.
    • Profits visible in an app but withdrawals repeatedly fail.

    Prevention:

    • Verify the broker/platform independently before depositing money.
    • Never make investment decisions solely from Telegram/WhatsApp tips.
    • Be suspicious of guaranteed returns and "limited-time" opportunities.
    • Start with regulated, verifiable platforms and understand what asset you are buying.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Fake Trading App

      -When you try to withdraw - app disappears

      Example: "Invest ₹10,000 - get ₹50,000 in 30 days" on fake Zerodha clone

    2. Crypto Scam

      3. -Fake cryptocurrency coins sold at low price

      Example: "Buy XYZ coin now at ₹1 - it will be ₹1,000 next month"

    3. Ponzi Scheme

      Example: Fake MLM scheme - refer 3 friends and earn ₹5,000 each

  5. Identity Theft (Financial)

    6. Identity Theft (Financial) is a type of cybercrime where criminals steal personal information such as name, date of birth, address, Social Security number, or bank account details to commit fraud or other crimes.

    How the fraud usually works:

    • Personal documents or financial credentials are obtained through phishing, data leaks, fake KYC requests, lost documents, or social engineering.
    • Criminals may use the information to attempt loans, SIM replacement, account opening, card fraud, or other financial activity.

    Warning signs:

    • Unexpected bank/loan/credit-card messages.
    • OTPs or KYC alerts for actions you did not initiate.
    • Your SIM suddenly stops receiving network service without explanation.
    • Unknown transactions or account-opening notifications.

    Prevention:

    • Share identity documents only when necessary and with verified organizations.
    • Mask/redact information when a full document is not required, where accepted.
    • Monitor bank accounts and credit/financial records for unfamiliar activity.
    • Secure your mobile number and important accounts with strong authentication.

    Immediate response:

    • Contact the bank/card issuer and mobile operator immediately when suspicious activity occurs.
    • Change affected passwords and secure email accounts first.
    • Keep evidence of unauthorized transactions and communications.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Aadhaar / PAN Misuse

      2. -Used to take loans or open bank accounts in your name

      Example: Victim gets loan recovery calls for loan they never took

    2. Credit Card Cloning

      3. -Card details stolen via skimmer at ATM

      Example: Transactions happening on your card in another city

    3. SIM Swap Fraud

      4. -Fraudster gets duplicate SIM of your number from store

      -Receives your OTPs and empties bank account

      Example: Your mobile stops working — bank account drained within hours

  6. Phishing

    7. Phishing is a type of cybercrime where attackers impersonate legitimate organizations or individuals to trick people into revealing sensitive information, such as usernames, passwords, credit card numbers, or other personal data, often through deceptive emails, messages, or websites.

    How phishing works:

    • The attacker impersonates a trusted organization, person, service, or authority.
    • A message creates urgency and directs the victim to a fake login page, malicious attachment, payment page, or phone number.
    • Information entered on the fake page can be captured and reused.

    Common psychological tricks:

    • Fear: "Your account will be blocked."
    • Urgency: "Verify within 30 minutes."
    • Reward: "You have won a prize."
    • Authority: "Police/bank/official notice."
    • Curiosity: "See this photo/video about you."

    How to detect a suspicious message:

    • Check the sender address, domain spelling, and link destination.
    • Be cautious of shortened or unfamiliar links.
    • Look for unusual grammar, unexpected attachments, and urgent demands.

    How to protect yourself:

    • Open the organization's official app/site independently instead of using the message link.

    Prevention:

    • Use multi-factor authentication.
    • Keep browsers and operating systems updated.
    • Never enter passwords or financial information on a page reached through an unexpected message.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Email Phishing

      2. -Fake email from "bank" with link to fake website

      Example: "Your HDFC account is suspended — click here to verify"

    2. Smishing (SMS Phishing)

      3. -Fraud SMS with malicious link

      Example: "Your KYC is incomplete — update now: bit.ly/hdfc-kyc"

    3. Vishing (Voice Phishing)

      4. -Phone call from fake bank/TRAI/police officer

      Example: "Your number will be disconnected in 2 hours — press 1 now"

    4. WhatsApp Phishing

      5. -Fake message from "official" number with link

      Example: Fake BSNL message: "Claim your free 84-day recharge here"

PRIVACY & PERSONAL DATA CRIMES

  1. Social Media Hacking

    2. Social Media Hacking is a type of cybercrime where attackers gain unauthorized access to a person's social media accounts, often to steal personal information, impersonate the victim, or commit fraud.

    How the hack usually works:

    • Phishing pages, reused passwords, leaked credentials, malicious apps, social engineering, or stolen sessions may be used.
    • After takeover, the attacker may change recovery details and contact the victim's friends to request money.

    Warning signs:

    • Login alerts from an unknown device/location.
    • Password-reset emails you did not request.
    • Unexpected profile, message, or privacy-setting changes.
    • Friends report receiving unusual messages from your account.

    Prevention:

    • Use a unique, strong password for every important account.
    • Enable multi-factor authentication.
    • Review active sessions and connected third-party applications.
    • Do not share verification codes or recovery links.

    Recovery:

    • Use the platform's official account-recovery process.
    • Secure the linked email account and phone number.
    • Inform contacts if messages were sent from the compromised account.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Password Guessing / Brute Force

      2. -Tries common passwords like birthdate, name123

      -Tries common passwords like birthdate, name123

      Example: Instagram hacked because password was "saurabh2001"

    2. Fake Login Page

      3. -Sends link to fake Instagram/Facebook login

      -You enter details — hacker gets your password

      Example: "Your account will be disabled — verify here" — fake page

    3. Session Hijacking

      4. -Steals your login session via public Wi-Fi

      Example: Logged into Facebook at café — someone else accessed your account

    4. Account Takeover for Scam

      5. -Hacks your account, messages your friends asking for money

      Example: "Bhai emergency hai — ₹2,000 bhej de" from your hacked ID

  2. Doxxing

    3. Doxxing is a type of cybercrime where an individual's private information, such as home address, phone number, or personal photos, is publicly shared online without their consent, often with the intent to harass, intimidate, or harm them.

    How doxxing happens:

    • Information may be collected from public social-media posts, data leaks, old profiles, public documents, or information shared by acquaintances.
    • Separate pieces of information can be combined to identify a person's home, workplace, family, or contact details.

    Risks:

    • Harassment, stalking, threats, impersonation, swatting-type abuse, or unwanted contact.
    • Family members may also become targets.

    Prevention:

    • Avoid publicly posting home addresses, personal phone numbers, routine schedules, tickets, IDs, or live locations.
    • Review old posts and public profile information.
    • Use privacy controls and remove unnecessary personal details from public bios.

    If doxxed:

    • Save evidence before requesting removal.
    • Report the post/account to the platform.
    • Tell trusted people if there is a realistic safety concern.
    • Avoid publicly escalating the conflict, which may attract additional harassment.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    • Personal Info Leak

      • -Posts your home address, phone number on public forums

      Example: Gamer trolled — enemy posts his address on Reddit

    • Workplace Harassment

      • -Sends fake complaints to your college/employer

      Example: Fake email to your college principal with false allegations

    • Family Targeting

      • -Contacts your parents or relatives with false information

      Example: Sends morphed photos to victim's family on WhatsApp

  3. Cyberstalking

    4. Cyberstalking is a type of cybercrime where an individual uses the internet, social media, email, or other digital communication tools to harass, intimidate, or threaten another person repeatedly over time.

    How cyberstalking develops:

    • It can begin with repeated unwanted messages and progress to monitoring, impersonation, location tracking, or contact through multiple accounts.
    • Public posts, location tags, shared photos, and compromised accounts can provide information to the stalker.

    Warning signs:

    • Repeated contact after blocking.
    • The person knows details you did not intentionally share.
    • New accounts repeatedly appear after blocking.
    • Suspicious location-sharing or account-login activity.

    Prevention:

    • Review location-sharing settings on phones and social platforms.
    • Remove unknown followers and connected applications.
    • Use strong authentication and device locks.
    • Avoid publicly posting real-time routines or locations.

    If the behaviour escalates:

    • Do not engage with the stalker or respond to threats.
    • Preserve evidence of messages, posts, and interactions.
    • Report the behavior to the platform and law enforcement if necessary.
    • Preserve a chronological record of incidents.
    • Tell trusted people and seek appropriate law-enforcement or platform support when threats or physical-safety concerns arise.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Location Tracking

      2. -Uses apps or social media check-ins to track your location

      Example: Stalker knows your college timing by monitoring Instagram stories

    2. Fake Profile Monitoring

      3. -Creates fake account to spy on your activity

      Example: Sends friend request from fake ID to see private posts

    3. Constant Messaging

      4. -Sends hundreds of messages daily from different numbers

      Example: Victim blocks — stalker creates new number and continues

    4. GPS Stalkerware

      5. -Installs hidden tracking app on victim's phone

      Example: Ex-partner installs app remotely — tracks every movement

  4. Wi-Fi / MitM Attack

    5. Wi-Fi / MitM Attack is a type of cybercrime where an attacker intercepts and potentially alters communication between two parties on a network, often to steal sensitive information, inject malicious content, or eavesdrop on private communications.

    How the attack works:

    • In a man-in-the-middle (MitM) attack, an attacker attempts to position themselves between a victim and an online service to observe, alter, or redirect communications.
    • Unsecured or malicious networks increase risk, particularly when a service does not properly protect traffic.

    Warning signs:

    • A Wi-Fi network with a generic name and no clear legitimate provider.
    • Unexpected certificate/security warnings.
    • Repeated redirects or unusual login pages.
    • Network behaviour that differs significantly from the legitimate service.

    Prevention:

    • Prefer trusted mobile data or known secure Wi-Fi for sensitive transactions.
    • Verify the exact network name with staff at airports, cafés, hotels, or colleges.
    • Ensure websites use HTTPS and do not ignore browser security warnings.
    • Keep devices and browsers updated.

    Important: Modern HTTPS/TLS provides strong protection when correctly implemented, so simply being on public Wi-Fi does not automatically mean an attacker can read all your traffic.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Fake Hotspot

      2. -Creates Wi-Fi named "Free_Airport_WiFi" — all traffic monitored

      Example: You connect at airport — hacker sees all websites you visit

    2. Packet Sniffing

      3. -Captures unencrypted data on public network

      Example: Your login credentials on HTTP website captured at café

    3. SSL Stripping

      4. -Downgrades HTTPS to HTTP, making data readable

      Example: Banking site forced to non-secure version — password stolen

  5. Malware / Spyware

    6. Malware / Spyware is a type of malicious software designed to infiltrate, damage, or gain unauthorized access to a computer system, network, or device, often to steal sensitive information, monitor user activity, or disrupt normal operations.

    How malware reaches normal users:

    • Malicious APKs, pirated software, fake updates, suspicious email attachments, malicious advertisements, and compromised websites are common delivery methods.
    • Social engineering is often used to persuade the victim to install or execute the malware.

    Warning signs:

    • Unexpected battery drain, overheating, crashes, pop-ups, or high data usage.
    • Unknown applications or accessibility permissions.
    • Security settings changing without permission.
    • Unusual SMS, calls, or account activity.

    Prevention:

    • Keep the operating system and applications updated.
    • Avoid pirated/cracked software and unknown APKs.
    • Install applications from trusted sources.
    • Review permissions and uninstall apps you no longer trust.
    • Use reputable device-security tools where appropriate.

    After infection:

    • Disconnect the affected device from networks if necessary to limit ongoing activity.
    • From a clean device, change important passwords and secure accounts.
    • Preserve evidence when the incident may require investigation.
    • Consider professional device cleanup/reset when compromise is suspected.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Fake APK Files:

      2. -Modified app files shared via WhatsApp/Telegram

      Example: "Free IPL streaming app" APK — actually steals contacts & SMS

    2. Spyware:

      3. -Runs silently in background, records everything

      Example: Pegasus spyware — reads messages, activates camera/mic

    3. Adware:

      4. -Floods device with ads, collects browsing data

      Example:> Free wallpaper app shows 20 ads/hour and tracks your activity

    4. Trojan:

      5. -Looks like a normal app but has hidden malicious code

      Example: Fake "battery saver" app actually sends your SMS to hacker

HARASSMENT & EMOTIONAL CRIMES

  2. Sextortion

    3. Sextortion is a form of sexual exploitation in which an individual is coerced or blackmailed into providing sexual images, videos, or favors, often under the threat of public exposure or harm.

    How sextortion commonly works:

    What to do:

    • Do not send more intimate material or continue negotiating unnecessarily.
    • Preserve messages, usernames, payment demands, and account details.
    • Block/report the offender after evidence is secured.
    • Tell a trusted person and seek appropriate law-enforcement/cybercrime support.

  3. Deepfake Fraud

    4. Deepfake Fraud is a type of cybercrime where artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning techniques are used to create realistic but fake audio, video, or images, often to deceive, manipulate, or defraud individuals or organizations.

    How deepfake fraud works:

    • AI can synthesize or alter faces, voices, images, and video.
    • Criminals can combine genuine photographs or audio with generated content to impersonate a person.

    Common uses:

    • Blackmail and non-consensual intimate imagery.
    • Fake endorsements or statements.
    • Family/emergency scams using cloned voices.
    • Fraudulent video calls or fake executive instructions.

    Warning signs:

    • Unexpected emergency requests for money.

    • A claim cannot be independently verified.

    Prevention:

    • Verify urgent requests using a second communication channel.
    • Use a known phone number rather than the number provided in the suspicious message.
    • Limit public access to high-quality personal photos/audio where practical.
    • Do not treat a video or voice recording as automatic proof of identity.

DEVICE & SYSTEM CRIMES

  1. Ransomware

    2. Ransomware is a type of malicious software that encrypts a victim's files or locks them out of their system, and the attacker demands a ransom payment in exchange for restoring access.

    How Ransomware Works:

    • Malware enters a device or network and may encrypt files, disrupt systems, and demand payment.
    • Modern ransomware can also involve theft of data before encryption, creating additional extortion pressure.

    Warning signs:

    • Files suddenly become inaccessible or receive unfamiliar extensions.
    • A ransom note appears.
    • Unusual mass file changes, system slowdown, or security alerts occur.

    Prevention:

    • Maintain regular backups, including an offline or otherwise isolated copy.
    • Keep operating systems, applications, and security software updated.
    • Do not open unexpected attachments or run unknown executables.
    • Use least-privilege accounts where possible.

    If infected:

    • Isolate the affected device/network to reduce spread.
    • Do not destroy evidence.
    • Inform the relevant IT/security team if it is a workplace or institutional device.
    • Report the incident and assess recovery from clean backups.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Email Attachment Ransomware

      2. -Opens infected PDF/Word file from unknown email

      Example:"Your invoice is attached" — opens it — all files locked

    2. Fake Software Update

      3. -Prompts fake Windows/Android update — installs ransomware

      Example: Pop-up: "Critical update required" — click — phone locked

    3. Ransom Note

      4. -Screen shows payment demand in Bitcoin

      Example: "Pay $300 in Bitcoin in 48 hours or your files will be deleted"

  2. Cryptojacking

    3. Cryptojacking is a type of cybercrime where an attacker secretly uses a victim's computing resources to mine cryptocurrency without their knowledge or consent.

    How cryptojacking works:

    • Malicious code uses CPU/GPU resources to mine cryptocurrency without permission.
    • It may arrive through compromised websites, malicious browser scripts, or infected applications.

    Common effects:

    • High CPU/GPU usage.
    • Overheating and fan noise.
    • Faster battery drain.
    • Slower application performance.
    • Higher electricity consumption on computers.

    Prevention:

    • Keep browsers, operating systems, and apps updated.
    • Avoid suspicious websites and unofficial software.
    • Remove unknown extensions and applications.
    • Investigate persistent high resource usage after installing a new application.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Browser-based Cryptojacking

      2. -Visiting a website runs hidden mining script

      Example: Open pirated movie site — CPU jumps to 100%, phone heats up

    2. App-based Cryptojacking

      3. -Fake app mines crypto silently in background

      Example: "Free VPN" app drains battery and slows phone — mining Monero

    3. Signs to detect it:

      4. -Device overheats, battery drains fast, phone becomes very slow

      Example: Phone was fine — after installing unknown app, it heats constantly

  3. Malware / Virus (Device Damage)

    4. Malware / Virus is a type of malicious software designed to infiltrate, damage, or gain unauthorized access to a computer system, network, or device, often to steal sensitive information, monitor user activity, or disrupt normal operations.

    Important distinction:

    • Virus: Usually attaches to a host file/program and spreads when the infected file is executed.
    • Worm: Can self-propagate across systems/networks without requiring the same host-file mechanism.
    • Trojan: Malicious software disguised as legitimate software.
    • Fileless malware: Uses legitimate system tools or memory-based techniques to reduce reliance on traditional files.

    Prevention:

    • Keep software patched and supported.
    • Use trusted application sources.
    • Scan removable USB drives before opening unknown content.
    • Maintain backups of important files.
    • Do not disable security protections simply to install suspicious software.

    Possible impact:

    • Data corruption or deletion.
    • Credential theft.
    • Unauthorized access.
    • System downtime.
    • Spread to other devices or networks.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Virus

      2. -Attaches to files and spreads, corrupting data

      Example: USB drive from friend infects your laptop — files get corrupted

    2. Worm

      3. -Self-replicates and spreads across networks automatically

      Example: One infected PC in office — all connected systems affected

    3. Fileless Malware

      4. -Runs in RAM without installing any file — hard to detect

      Example: No app installed but banking credentials stolen via browser

IDENTITY & IMPERSONATION CRIMES

  1. Identity Theft (Personal)

    2. Personal Identity Theft is a type of cybercrime in which criminals steal and misuse a person’s personal information without permission to impersonate them, access accounts, or commit fraud.

    How personal identity theft happens:

    • Criminals collect names, photographs, identity documents, phone numbers, addresses, or other identifiers from leaks, social media, phishing, or stolen devices.
    • The information may be used to create accounts, fake profiles, fraudulent documents, or deceptive communications.

    Warning signs:

    • Unknown accounts or services connected to your identity.
    • People contact you about messages or profiles you did not create.
    • Unexplained verification codes or identity-verification requests.
    • Personal information appears publicly where you did not post it.

    Prevention:

    • Limit public personal information.
    • Protect identity documents and avoid unnecessary sharing.
    • Use strong passwords and MFA.
    • Regularly review important accounts and digital profiles.

    Response:

    • Report impersonation to the affected platform/service.
    • Secure your accounts and documents.
    • Keep copies of evidence showing the unauthorized use.
    • Definition: Stealing someone's personal identity details to impersonate them or misuse their name.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Document Forgery

      2. -Creates fake ID using your Aadhaar/PAN photo

      Example: Fake driving license made with your photo but criminal's name

    2. Social Identity Theft

      3. -Uses your name, photos to create fake online presence

      Example: Your photos used to create dating profile on Tinder without consent

  2. Fake Profile Creation

    3. Fake Profile Creation is a type of cybercrime in which an individual creates a false online identity using someone else's real name, photos, or personal information, often to deceive, manipulate, or defraud others.

    How fake profiles are used:

    • Criminals copy names, profile photographs, bios, and other public information.
    • The fake account may be used for financial fraud, harassment, reputation damage, romance scams, or social engineering.

    Warning signs:

    • A duplicate profile with a similar username and copied photos.
    • New accounts with little history but many claims of familiarity.
    • Requests for money, OTPs, personal information, or private photographs.
    • Friends report receiving unusual messages from a profile pretending to be you.

    Prevention:

    • Keep sensitive profile information private.
    • Use platform reporting and impersonation-protection features.
    • Tell contacts which account is genuine if an impersonation appears.
    • Avoid accepting requests solely because the profile uses a familiar name/photo.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Impersonation for Fraud

      2. -Fake profile of you used to borrow money from your contacts

      Example: Fake "Saurabh Patkar" profile messages your friends for ₹1,000

    2. Reputation Damage

      3. -Fake profile posts offensive content in your name

      Example: Fake Twitter account in your name posts communal content

    3. Catfishing

      4. -Completely fake identity used to emotionally trap someone

      Example: Fake handsome profile on Instagram — builds relationship — then extorts money

  3. Email / Social Spoofing

    4. Email / Social Spoofing is a type of cybercrime in which an attacker falsifies the sender information in email or social media messages to make it appear as though the communication is coming from a trusted source, often to deceive recipients into taking harmful actions, such as revealing sensitive information or clicking on malicious links.

    How spoofing differs from hacking:

    • Spoofing primarily involves making communication appear to come from a trusted source; the genuine account itself may not have been compromised.
    • Attackers can imitate names, email addresses, caller IDs, logos, profile photographs, or social-media identities.

    Warning signs:

    • A request is unusual for the supposed sender.
    • The message asks for money, passwords, OTPs, gift cards, or sensitive files.
    • The sender insists on secrecy or urgency.
    • Email domains or social handles contain subtle spelling changes.

    Prevention:

    • Verify unusual requests through a separate trusted channel.
    • Inspect the complete email address rather than only the displayed name.
    • Organizations can use email authentication controls such as SPF, DKIM, and DMARC to reduce certain forms of email spoofing.
    • Never treat caller ID alone as proof of identity.

    Sub-topics & Examples:

    1. Email Spoofing

      2. -Sends email that appears to come from your boss/bank

      Example: Email from "hr@yourcompany.com" asks to transfer funds — fake

    2. Caller ID Spoofing

      3. -Fake call appears from real government/bank number

      Example: Call shows "TRAI" on screen but it's a scammer

    3. Social Media Spoofing

      4. -Creates account nearly identical to a celebrity or brand

      Example: Fake "Paytm Support" account on Twitter asks for OTP
